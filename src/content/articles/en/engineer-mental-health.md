This article is not for diagnosing or treating anything. If things stay hard for a while, use the support options at the end.

## Burnout comes from workload

The World Health Organization (WHO) describes burn-out as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is treated as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. It has three features:

- Feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion
- Increased mental distance from your job, or negative or cynical feelings about it
- Reduced professional efficacy

The cause is placed in “workplace stress that was not managed”, not in the person’s weakness. You can’t prevent it with temperament or grit, so the fix has to include the workload itself.

## Loads that are common for engineers

The nature of the work lets some loads build up easily:

- **Interruptions**: chat and incidents break your focus again and again
- **Work that doesn’t end**: bugs, dependency updates and reviews; clear one and the next arrives
- **On-call**: you don’t know when you’ll be called, even outside work hours
- **Feeling behind**: technology moves fast and AI tools keep multiplying, so it feels like you’ll be left behind if you stop learning
- **Hard-to-see results**: operations and maintenance go unnoticed the better they work

You can’t reduce any of these by effort alone. It helps to list them and ask whether the way work is done, or the team set-up, can take some of them away.

It helps to split the response into four steps: notice, step back, talk, and recover.

## Examples of signs

Treat these as signs if they last two weeks or more:

- You feel drained in the morning, before work has even started
- Work that used to be interesting no longer holds you
- Small mistakes increase and decisions take longer
- Your sleep is disturbed (trouble falling asleep, waking early, not feeling rested)
- Replying to colleagues feels like a chore and you start avoiding it

Don’t brush it off as “just tired”. Jot down a line with the date, and you can see the change when you look back.

## Small habits for stepping back

Even without a long break, small daily changes lower the load:

- **Decide when work ends**: turn off work notifications at a set time
- **Protect focus time**: put work blocks on the calendar, separate from the time you take interruptions
- **Say no, or push back**: make priorities visible, and say early that not everything can be done
- **Look after the body first**: sleep, meals and light exercise work before your mood does
- **Schedule time outside work**: keep work from slipping into the free gaps

## Don’t carry it alone

If it stays hard, don’t try to manage it by yourself; talk to someone. In rough order of how close they are:

1. A colleague or friend you trust
2. Your manager (raise it as a question of workload and team set-up)
3. The company’s occupational physician or support desk
4. A medical clinic (psychosomatic medicine or psychiatry) or a public support line

In Japan there are free lines you can use (they answer in Japanese):

- Kokoro no Kenko Sodan Tongo Dial: 0570-064-556 (connects you to the service in your area)
- Yorisoi Hotline: 0120-279-338 (open 24 hours)

If your life feels in danger, call 110 or 119 without hesitation. Outside Japan, use the local emergency number and mental health lines.

> Asking for help is not an overreaction; it is an ordinary way to keep doing this work for a long time. The earlier you talk, the more options you still have.
